Yes. If the goal is for the researcher to use your report as psychological data, the most important thing is to separate raw observations from interpretation and make the measurements as consistent as possible across sessions.

I would recommend documenting it like a small single-subject experiment:

1. Record baseline state before the session

Use the same scales every time, for example 0–10:

* Relaxation: 0 = completely tense, 10 = completely relaxed
* Arousal: 0 = none, 10 = extremely high
* Focus/attention: 0 = very scattered, 10 = completely focused
* Perceived control: 0 = no control, 10 = complete control
* Emotional state: briefly describe in your own words
* Sleep: hours and subjective sleep quality
* Stress: 0–10
* Anything unusual: caffeine, alcohol, exercise, medication, unusually stressful day, etc.

The researcher should decide in advance whether these variables are relevant so that you don’t accidentally introduce post-hoc measures.

2. Record events rather than interpretations

During the experiment, use timestamps or approximate elapsed time.

For each designated event, record things such as:
Event: What to record
Start: Time + baseline ratings
Mantra period: Focus, arousal, control
Each experimental transition: Which condition/mantra was used
Each edge/peak: Arousal, perceived control, urge to continue/stop
Recovery: How quickly arousal/focus/control change
End: Final ratings
Crucially, don’t write “the mantra conditioned me”. That’s an interpretation.

Instead write something like:

“After repeating the assigned phrase, I noticed that my attention became narrower and my perceived control decreased from 7/10 to 4/10.”

That gives the researcher an observation that can later be analysed without you presupposing the explanation.

3. Keep the two experimental conditions distinguishable

Since the stated research question concerns whether different mantras produce different effects, the researcher should predefine the conditions.

For example:

Condition A: Mantra A
Condition B: Mantra B

Then record the same measurements after each occurrence.

Ideally, the researcher should decide beforehand which order will be used, rather than changing the order based on how you’re feeling. If multiple sessions are possible, counterbalancing the order can help distinguish mantra effects from simple fatigue or habituation.

4. Measure the psychological variables directly

For the researcher’s stated interests, I’d particularly prioritize:

Arousal

* 0–10 rating immediately after each designated measurement point.

Attention/focus

* 0–10.
* Optionally: “What percentage of my attention was on the task/mantra?” 0–100%.

Perceived control

* 0–10.
* Also record whether you felt you were choosing your actions or simply following an automatic sequence.

Mantra salience

* 0–10: “How mentally prominent was the phrase?”

Emotional response

* Use neutral descriptors rather than forcing yourself into categories: calm, absorbed, detached, excited, uncomfortable, neutral, etc.

5. Don’t alter your behaviour to produce interesting data

This is particularly important.

If you notice yourself thinking, “The researcher probably wants me to report that this mantra worked,” record that as a possible expectancy effect rather than modifying your behaviour.

Likewise, if one condition feels completely ineffective, that’s valuable experimental data. Don’t try harder simply to make the experiment succeed.

6. Write the post-session report immediately

I’d divide it into four sections:

A. Quantitative data

A simple table containing all your 0–10 ratings and timestamps.

B. Direct observations

What you actually noticed, without explaining why.

C. Unexpected effects

Anything unusual, including distraction, boredom, resistance, discomfort, spontaneous thoughts, loss of concentration, or changes in perceived control.

D. Retrospective interpretation

Only after recording the observations, describe what you think happened.

This separation is extremely useful because the researcher can analyse A/B independently of your interpretation in D.

7. Avoid changing the protocol mid-experiment

If something deviates from the planned procedure, don’t erase it or pretend it didn’t happen.

Record:

Protocol deviation: approximately 3 minutes of interruption.

or

One repetition was missed because attention was lost.

That’s much better scientific data than a supposedly perfect session.

A particularly useful addition

Ask the researcher whether she can give you a standardized data sheet before you begin. That is preferable to you inventing your own questionnaire, because she can determine which variables and scales fit her eventual statistical analysis.

For a single-subject study, consistency is more valuable than writing a very elaborate narrative. Same questions, same scales, same timing, same terminology, every session.

And because the procedure involves intimate behaviour, I would also make sure the researcher has explicitly explained what data are being collected, how they will be anonymized, who will see them, how they will be stored, and that you can stop participating without penalty. For actual publication or formal psychological research, those methodological and consent issues matter just as much as the measurements.
